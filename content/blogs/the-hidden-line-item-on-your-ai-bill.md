We've spent the last 3 or 4 years obsessing over developer velocity, shaving seconds off CI/CD pipelines and hours off feature delivery. That speed comes with a fee nobody budgets for. To see who pays it, look at two Thursday evenings, eight years apart.

## Alice's autonomy

It's a pretty Thursday evening in 2018: Alice just shipped a moderately difficult business feature after two sprints of hard work.

She had been balancing the load between herself and her team, creating issues, rigorously reviewing PRs and whatnot. Alice felt on top of the world and had a short-lived god complex.

Alice comes home and tells her family about her work; although they don't understand a word she says, they feel happy for her. She feels good about herself and her work, and she gets a good night's sleep.

## Bob's automation

Roll the camera forward to another pretty Thursday evening, this time in 2026. Bob's pull request to ship the product's most critical feature is finally approved by an AI code review bot, after the bot leaves 20 article-length comments.

Bob used AI to write the feature entirely on his own. He used an AI workflow to resolve comments and auto-generate replies, and his delivery speed went up tenfold. His teammates, busy vibe-coding their own features, didn't bother reviewing his code; one simply stamped an "LGTM" after the bot greenlit the PR.

Bob has just delivered the most important feature of the product, yet he doesn't feel on top of the world. It feels like just another transactional day. He goes home and doesn't talk about his work.

Bob sits alone and stares into the abyss. Shipping an important feature doesn't excite him, and his work gives him no sense of accomplishment or novelty. He questions his worth and existence as an engineer and has trouble falling asleep.

## What the speed costs

Mainstream LLM APIs cost between $0.03 and $10.00 per million input tokens, and output tokens generally cost 3 to 6 times more. The expense I'm talking about is far heavier than that, and much harder to accept.

We measure cloud bills in dollars and cents. Our hyper-automated workflows cost us something else as well, and we pay it slowly, out of our biological motivation.

What Bob felt that Thursday evening was more than ordinary burnout. The reward system his brain evolved was slowly atrophying.

For hundreds of thousands of years, the human dopamine system has worked on a simple principle: effort comes before gratification. The high Alice felt in 2018 was her brain's payout for two sprints of friction, uncertainty and long problem-solving, with the shipped code as the trigger.

When you sit through the discomfort of deep thinking, your brain rewards you for solving the problem with a real sense of mastery and self-worth. Generative AI destroys that friction and cuts the effort off from the output completely.

When an engineer can vibe code a major feature in hours from natural language prompts, the prefrontal cortex is effectively bypassed. The brain reaches an endpoint (a merged PR, a shipped feature, a green checkmark) with almost no cognitive labour. Without the struggle that should come first, the brain files the result as a non-event. The dopamine high flattens into a grey baseline, and you end up staring into the abyss even though you're ten times faster.

Worse, AI tools replace earned achievement with a steady stream of small stimuli. Every prompt gives an unpredictable response, like a pull on a slot machine, and each one releases a small dose of anticipatory dopamine. That keeps us iterating in loops that never end in deep satisfaction. When bots and solo coding replace collaboration and peer review, we also lose the social validation from colleagues that grounds our identity at work. And as constant digital stimulation wears down our impulse control, our capacity for "productive frustration", the state where mastery and new ideas come from, drops close to zero.

When we calculate the ROI of artificial intelligence, we tally latency, token counts and developer velocity. The hidden line item on that invoice is human agency. Heavy AI use wears down our drive to create, to push through hard problems and to feel proud of the work we leave behind, and that costs far more than the API bill at the end of the month.

## So, where do we go from here?

We don't need to throw away our tools or pretend AI hasn't changed how we work. If we want to stay whole as engineers and as people, though, we have to protect our cognitive friction as if it were a non-renewable resource.

Efficiency measures how well machines run. It says nothing about whether the people using them are fulfilled. If you want that 2018 feeling back, the late-night pride of having beaten something difficult, you have to choose hard things on purpose. Step away from the prompt box, work out a complex architectural boundary on paper, or debate a design in person with a teammate instead of deferring to a bot.

AI can write our boilerplate. It shouldn't get to write off our agency too. Part of the return on building software has always been who we become while building it.
